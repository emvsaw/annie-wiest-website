*Rae*

Rae was halfway through the bridge of her final song, singing to eight thousand people in the open-air Telluride amphitheater, when Leesa's voice cut into the private channel of her in-ear monitor.

"Confirm extra coverage at the south gate and main entrance. Same protocol." A pause. Then, for Rae alone: "Security briefing after the set. Green room."

Not now. Rae kept singing.

South gate. The crowded seats sloped up toward a sky that was too blue, too thin; altitude made the world feel sharp and distant at the same time.

Eight thousand faces in the late-August gold, a single breathing thing. She made it through the bridge and hit every note. The crowd took the air with her like in Omaha, in Charlotte, in Boise, in every city where she'd played since April. Her left in-ear monitor was running hot in the mids again. She'd mentioned it at soundcheck.

The venue tech had nodded, and by the second song it had drifted right back. Fixing it would cost ten minutes they didn't have, so she compensated without thinking.

The band held the bridge; her guitar hung quiet on its strap, and her right thumb moved over the calluses of her left fingertips, one by one, then back. It was a motion she'd repeated since she was sixteen. Back then she'd stood in Papa-ji's kitchen in Asheville, trying to find a C chord that wouldn't buzz, the harmonium in the corner while her father listened without saying anything, which was how he said everything. She could not think of that kitchen now without imagining the morning almost fifteen years later, when he died at the table, one hand still on his chai mug.

That same harmonium was in the equipment bay tonight, three rows back from the stage in its case. It had been in its case a long time, with a sticker on it she could not look at without thinking about the hand that had put it there.

She gave them the chorus, full-throated. No cracks. The scar of eighteen months in her voice was audible to her on the sustained notes and not to anyone else: a thinness, the resonance she'd never fully gotten back. And then, the audience gave her something back she could not use. Adoration was a currency from a country she did not live in anymore.

That song had no soul left. Somewhere around Omaha it had emptied out. Her body still knew every breath, every pause, every note. The rest of her didn't.

"Thank you, Telluride."

Seventeenth city. Same words. She walked stage left before the last note had finished traveling out into the golden evening, guitar low in her right hand, and she did not take a bow. She had stopped taking bows two years ago. It had started as a choice and it was a fact about her now.

A life with staff and protocols moved entirely without her. Rae just had to show up and sing, and she had, three hundred and forty times.

She had not updated her bio in over a year. The label had. It said about her that she was fearless and unflinching and Rae had read it once on an airport screen and thought _that is a person I would like to meet._

<p class="scene-break">* * *</p>

The first thing Rae saw backstage was Leesa's face, and she knew.

It was the look that meant a problem had not really been managed. Only contained.

Backstage was hot on one side, cold on the other, evening air coming through the gaps in the staging structure. Sweat cooled fast at her hairline.

Leesa held out an opened water bottle toward Rae's hand, a practiced gesture to keep her moving.

Rae took it.

"Level four," Leesa said. "They have someone at the south gate."

Level four meant a credible, targeted threat: someone had tried to reach her before and knew enough about her movements to make the usual security plan useless. The same protocol as Denver. As Boise. Rae kept her face neutral, her pace steady, the cold plastic of the bottle the only thing she could clearly feel. The internal checklist tried to start running, but she stopped it. Running the list was what you did when you were afraid, and she was not afraid. She was managing.

She opened the green room door.

<p class="scene-break">* * *</p>

She had been awake since five. Adrenaline still buzzed under her skin, but her hands stayed ice-cold. Fluorescent lights leached the green room gray. Clara spoke of the man and of a letter venue security had intercepted. It had named Rae's hotel.

"Same stalker?" Leesa asked.

Clara looked at Rae before she answered. "We believe so."

Rae did not ask what else the letter said.

The woman near the door watched her with steady, competent eyes and rough hands. Rae's right thumb circled her left fingertips once before she forced it still.

Clara was still talking, now about options: hotel, the tour bus, a flight tonight. Rae's ears were still ringing from the in-ear monitors. She was thinking about the six remaining shows, Portland, Seattle, Sacramento, two in Los Angeles, Austin, and what canceling them would cost her. Not in money.

The stranger near the door shifted her weight. Her bearing was law enforcement. Off-duty maybe, in those worn jeans and that nondescript fleece jacket, but training did not go off-duty. She already knew where everyone was standing without appearing to check.

Then the woman stepped away from the door. "Step out, please. Let me talk to her."

She said it evenly, not checking whether anyone would go. Nobody asked her who she was. Clara went first. The two venue security followed. Leesa nodded at her and was also gone. The door closed after the last person.

Bass frequencies from the next act's soundcheck came through the plywood and staging material. The fluorescent lights hummed and clicked.

The woman crouched to her eye level, boots scraping soft on the pressed-flat dirt as she settled on her heels.

"You are safe right now."

The words landed like cool water on hot skin, steady.

_No one has been this close to my face in months without wanting a piece of the stage version._

The woman's hands rested easy on her knees. Her weight settled back on her heels. She wasn't filling the silence. She was in it.

"Tell me what you actually need."

In a folding chair under fluorescent light in a room that smelled of packed dirt and stale snacks, Rae sat across from a woman she'd never met who was asking the one question no one had asked in a very long time.

Rae considered the woman's hands, steady on her knees, rough at the fingertips like someone who worked with them. For one disorienting second, she wondered what those hands would feel like on skin that wasn't part of the job. She hadn't had a thought like that in months.

She didn't know what she needed. She could not remember the last time she had known.
